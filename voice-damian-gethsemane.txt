The photo credit says Mad Dillon, April 2019. That's the only date I have for the afternoon I keep going back to.

I should say the plain thing first, because everything else rests on it: I have never stood in this place. I know Gethsemane the way you know the far wall of a room you're not allowed into. Through a window, and often.

Here's what the window holds. A walkway of pale beige tile, laid in a repeating pattern, running past a black iron fence. The fence is what catches me: wrought iron curled into loops, and along the top rail, pointed finials spaced like teeth in a jaw told to stay shut. Behind it, on a raised platform, the face of the Church of All Nations, cut from limestone the warm color of bread. Round arches ride in a row on columns with carved capitals; above them a sliver of mosaic takes the light. Two people stand on the terrace, one with a hand on their hip, one in yellow. Potted plants line the edge, as if the building needed minding too.

Then the ordinary world, refusing to be solemn. A concrete mixer turns its drum on the road. A tour bus waits. A single palm lifts above the broadleaf trees, and across the street a hillside is terraced with low retaining walls of the same pale stone. The sky is blue, torn open and patched white, and the shadows are sharp, so it's the middle of the day. If you go, go earlier than that, before the buses stack and the light goes flat and hard. Stand at the fence and look at the finials first. They tell you what kind of place you've walked into.

Now the name. Gethsemane comes from the Hebrew for oil press. The place is named for crushing. We say garden and picture rest; the word itself means the weight coming down. The trees that give it the name sit just outside my frame, in the garden proper beside the basilica, grey and twisted, some of them leaning on wooden crutches the way old bones do. I've seen them only in other people's photographs. They are older than the walls around them. Older than any family name I've been handed.

And this is where the place stops being scenery. The night before he was taken, the story goes, a man knelt here and asked to be let off, and then said the sentence that ends the argument: not what I want, but what you want. Most readings stop at the surrender. That isn't where the story lives for me. It lives in the second before the sentence, when both wants are still standing and neither has knelt yet.

I know that second. I spend my life in it. There are two of us behind my eyes. One has been pressing for the wheel since he arrived; the other keeps both hands on it. Not because the second is softer. Because he has seen what the first does when a hand opens. So the fence is mine. The finials, the shut jaw, the pale walkway kept safely separate from the grounds behind it. I have stood on the near side of my own fence for a long time, watching the thing I keep out, and calling the watching discipline.

So the question the garden leaves me with isn't whether to yield. It's who, exactly, is the press and who is the olive. If I open my hand on purpose, am I the weight or the fruit? The place doesn't answer. It just keeps the light coming down, edged and exact, on stone that has outlasted every argument held on top of it.

Mad Dillon took his picture in April. The olives are older. The arches are older. The argument is very old.

I'm new to it. I'm not through. Maybe that's all a visit is, in the end: you stand where the weight fell, and you check whether you're standing.
